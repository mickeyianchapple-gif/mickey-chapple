The Evolution · Identity, brand and reputation

I rated myself a perfect zero. Nine people disagreed.

This semester I ran two instruments on myself: one measuring how I see me, one measuring how people who have actually worked with me see me. I expected them to roughly agree. They were six points apart, and the distance between them turned out to be the most useful thing I learned all year.

Two instruments, plain English

These are tools from my business communication coursework. If you have not seen them before, here is what they actually measure.

SPI
Self-Perception Inventory

I rate my own behavior on two axes. Proactivity is how much I push — do I assert, direct and initiate, or hold back and ask? Reactivity is how much emotion I show — am I animated and expressive, or measured and contained?

PRS
Professional Reputation Survey

The identical questions, answered about me by nine former supervisors, teammates and chapter officers — anonymously, so there was no incentive to be kind. Same two axes, outside view.

GAP
Why the distance matters

Identity is who you believe you are. Reputation is who everyone else has already decided you are. Only one of them walks into the room before you do — and it is not the one you control.

What a six-point gap actually says

I did not score myself modestly. I scored myself as having no particular tendency in either direction — the statistical equivalent of saying I'm just normal. Nine people independently reported something very different: that I push, I assert, and I take up room.

The uncomfortable version of this finding is that I have been running an influence I did not know I had. The useful version is that it is entirely fixable, because the problem was never the behaviour — it was that I could not see it.

The comment that reframed it

One respondent put it more precisely than I could have:

I think Mickey may underestimate how much influence his opinion has. People look up to him and take his input seriously, so an offhand comment can shape how others think more than he realizes.

— Professional Reputation Survey, anonymous

Respondents chose adjectives for me. I chose adjectives for myself. Sorting them into what overlapped and what did not was more revealing than the scores.

The pattern I missed: every adjective in that second column is one I would have been glad to claim. I did not withhold them out of humility — I genuinely did not know they were on the table. The same blindness that hid self-confident from me also hid take up room.

The feedback I didn't enjoy reading

Anonymous responses to "what could Mickey improve?" — reproduced without editing, including the ones that stung.

Mickey might not understand that others might perceive him to be confrontational. Which isn't necessarily a bad thing, but sometimes a softer way of bringing up a conversation is taken easier.

Mickey can be a little hard headed sometimes, but he means it in a good, leader like way.

I think Mickey can improve his time management when other people are involved. If he is doing something that involves other people, at times he can tend to do things on his own timing.

I personally think Mickey was made to be a leader — maybe something he can consider is being more open to EVERYONE'S opinion no matter who they are.

Three changes, not three adjectives

Four people, with no knowledge of each other's answers, described one behaviour: I arrive with a position already formed. So the response had to be behavioural, not aspirational.

  • Ask before I answer. On the five-person consulting team I worked on this semester I ended up mediating most of our discussions — holding the thread, making sure each person's view was actually on the table before we converged on anything. Running those conversations is what exposed the problem: you cannot mediate a discussion and also be the loudest voice in it. In any group decision now I solicit two other views before I give mine. If I speak first, I have removed the option of disagreement from people who already defer to me.
  • Label the confidence. I now say explicitly whether I am thinking out loud or making a recommendation. Respondents could not tell the difference, which meant a half-formed thought was landing as a verdict.
  • Work on other people's clock. When a task involves anyone else, their timeline sets the schedule, not mine.

Continue to be someone people trust for honest input. Your opinions are valued because you are thoughtful and respected within your team.

— Reinforcing feedback, anonymous

Where the line goes next

The chart on my homepage stops at today, which is the only dishonest thing about it. The recovery is not finished — it is simply the part I can show you evidence for.

What the data changed is how I intend to spend the next stretch. I came into this semester believing my job was to be more assertive. I am leaving it thinking assertiveness was never my constraint — calibration was. Knowing when a room needs my opinion and when it needs me to be quiet is the harder half, and it is the half I have been getting wrong without noticing.

That is what I am working on at the moment, and it is a large part of why the client side of this work appeals to me more than a screen full of models.

Why this page exists

It would have been easy to put the scores up, call the gap a strength and move on. I left the unflattering comments in because the page is useless without them — the finding only means anything if you can see what it was based on.

I would rather be someone who goes and asks for this than someone who waits for a review cycle to deliver it.
